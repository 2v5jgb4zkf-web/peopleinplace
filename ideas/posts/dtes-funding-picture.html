Transparency, scale, and the DTES funding picture

23 August 2026 · Shelby

A new public portal — hrinc.org — has compiled disclosed federal, provincial, and municipal grants to roughly 310 registered societies and charities operating in or focused on Vancouver’s Downtown Eastside for the period 2015–2025. The aggregate figure it surfaces is large: on the order of several billion dollars in logged public grants across more than 10,000 allocations.

The site functions primarily as a transparency tool. It organizes organizations by sector (housing and shelter, harm reduction and healthcare, mental health, food, Indigenous services, and others), shows funder splits, and makes individual profiles and CSV exports available. For the largest operators the data typically shows heavy reliance on provincial contracts, with smaller federal and city shares. A relatively small number of established housing, health, and support providers account for a substantial portion of the total.

This kind of aggregation is useful. Public grant and contract data has long been scattered across CRA filings, ministry reports, and municipal lists. Making the disclosed flows searchable and comparable lowers the barrier to basic questions about concentration, trends, and the overall public commitment to one neighbourhood.

It is also limited by design. The portal tracks disclosed grants, not total organizational revenue, private philanthropy, capital expenditures, or outcomes. It does not measure housing stability, health indicators, employment exits, or reductions in visible disorder. Without those paired metrics, the numbers describe the scale of investment more clearly than they evaluate its effectiveness.

A few observations follow from the data as presented:

  • Provincial funding dominates the largest contracts. This reflects the structure of BC Housing, health, and related programs more than municipal decision-making.
  • The density of organizations is high. Specialization can be valuable; fragmentation and overlapping mandates can also create coordination costs and dilute accountability.
  • Decades of rising public expenditure have coincided with persistent challenges in the same geography. That fact alone does not prove waste or success. It does make rigorous outcome tracking and independent evaluation more important, not less.

Transparency of this kind is a necessary condition for better decisions, not a sufficient one. The practical questions remain the same whether one views the current model as under-resourced, misallocated, or both: What results are being purchased with these dollars? How are those results measured and compared? Where is the feedback loop between spending and measurable improvement in the lives of residents and the functioning of the neighbourhood?

Clearer data on inputs is a start. Clearer data on outcomes, and the institutional willingness to act on both, will determine whether the next decade looks different from the last.
