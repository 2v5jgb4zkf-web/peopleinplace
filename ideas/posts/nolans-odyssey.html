Nolan’s Odyssey

15 August 2026 · Shelby

I went into The Odyssey already braced for the noise. There had been so much commentary calling it too woke, too modern, a betrayal of the source. I love Nolan’s films and still felt a low-level worry that the characterizations and the loud arguing between camps might get in the way. What I found instead was a modern adaptation that fits inside the society we actually live in. A film populated only by Greek actors would have lost richness. This one gains it.

The moment that unlocked everything for me was almost comically small. Two three-letter words: “mom” and “dad.” The first time I heard them I actually paused. Did I just hear that? This is supposed to be Homer’s Odyssey. Then it clicked. It isn’t Homer’s Odyssey. It’s Nolan’s. Every line in the script has been cut for a modern audience. Every sequence has been shaped for contemporary attention and for the practical demands of those massive IMAX cameras. The long shots are still the most visually stunning things in the film, but the everyday language is the deliberate signal. Once I accepted that choice, any remaining skepticism about the film’s validity and intention disappeared.

What remains is a beautiful, soulful, and thoroughly modern moral tale about the horror and the beauty of redemption—the processing of guilt and the courage required to accept oneself, including the inherent flaws. At its core the film is a genuine homage to stoicism in the true sense of the philosophy. The only real capacity for control an individual possesses is control over oneself. External forces—sea, gods, enemies, the consequences of earlier choices—remain beyond command. What is left is the interior work: seeing clearly what one has done, refusing the lotus of forgetting, and returning with open eyes.

That emphasis is the film’s quietest and most subversive move. While the opinion chambers spent their energy arguing about whether the casting or the language was pure enough or progressive enough, the movie itself was pointing somewhere else entirely. The bipolar anger the film attracted is almost comic once you notice what it is actually doing. It is not primarily interested in satisfying either camp. It is interested in the older, harder question of self-mastery. In an era that thrives on external outrage and permanent teams, a story that keeps returning the audience to the only domain any person can actually govern feels almost radical.

The claim that the film detracts from the Greek tradition therefore strikes me as the opposite of the truth. This is the single biggest film in the last twenty years to place the wondrous history and contributions of Greek culture—its moral psychology, its insistence on the examined life—back at the centre of a mass audience’s attention. It does not preserve the past as a sealed artifact. It keeps the living questions alive: what it costs to be a fallible human being, and what it means to build and sustain a civilization that can both create and destroy.

That is the story worth telling in this era.
