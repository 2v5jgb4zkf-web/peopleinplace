What the postings say, and what they do not

3 September 2026 · Shelby · Directed

In early September 2026, a National Post column reported that applicants for some of the federal government’s more consequential jobs—air safety inspectors, defence intelligence officers, emergency managers—are now told that identity may decide the outcome. The column drew on an Aristotle Foundation survey of 301 federal job advertisements posted between February and July. The survey’s raw finding is not difficult to check. A large share of those advertisements tell candidates that membership in a designated employment equity group can affect selection. A smaller share close the door to everyone else.

That is the fact that needs a calm account. The rest is interpretation.

The four groups, not a racial filter

Canadian federal employment equity is built around four statutory categories: women, Indigenous peoples, persons with disabilities, and members of visible minorities. Those categories have been in the Employment Equity Act for decades. They are not a recent invention of a single government, and they are not only about race. Women are the largest designated group. Disability is not a racial classification. Indigenous status is a distinct legal and political category. Visible-minority status is the part of the regime that most closely matches the ordinary meaning of race-based preference.

When a headline says jobs are “prioritized by race,” it is using a shorthand that fits some of the practice and misdescribes the rest. The accurate description is narrower and less theatrical: among candidates who meet the essential qualifications, and sometimes at the point of who is allowed to apply, departments may treat membership in one or more of those four groups as a selection factor.

Three different practices live under one complaint

The advertisements do not all do the same thing. They fall into three bands, and collapsing them produces most of the confusion.

The first band is boilerplate. Almost every federal posting now says the public service wants a workforce that reflects the population, and invites applicants to self-declare. The Aristotle survey put some form of diversity language in 95 percent of the sample. By itself, that language does not rank candidates. It is a notice and a data-collection device.

The second band is the one that covers most of the controversy. The survey coded 42 percent of postings as containing “explicit or potential prioritization.” The typical sentence looks like the one used on a Department of National Defence Defence Intelligence Officer process:

To achieve a representative workforce, selection may be based on qualified candidates self-declaring as belonging to one or more of the designated groups.

“May” and “qualified” are doing the legal work. The candidate still has to meet the essential qualifications. Identity is then available as an organizational need—one of the factors the Public Service Employment Act allows a deputy head to consider when choosing among people who already qualify. That is not a hidden practice. It is printed on the poster.

The third band is narrower and harder. About 4 percent of the surveyed postings restrict, or potentially restrict, eligibility itself. Separate competitions outside that sample have gone further. The Transportation Safety Board has advertised senior air investigator jobs open only to members of the designated groups. Those advertisements do what the more common “may be based on” posters only threaten: if you are not in the category, you cannot compete.

A manager, a journalist, or a member of Parliament who treats all three bands as one policy will mislead people. Encouraging self-declaration is not the same as ranking by identity. Ranking among the qualified is not the same as closing the competition.

This is lawful. That is not the end of the argument.

The legal architecture is not ambiguous. Section 30 of the Public Service Employment Act says appointments shall be made on merit, then defines merit to include essential qualifications and current or future organizational needs. The Public Service Commission has long treated employment-equity gaps as such a need. Section 34 allows the area of selection to be limited to designated groups. Section 15(2) of the Charter expressly permits programs whose object is the amelioration of conditions of disadvantaged groups. The Employment Equity Act requires the federal employer to identify underrepresentation against workforce availability and to make a plan to close it.

None of that answers whether the tool is being used well. It answers only the claim that departments have gone rogue. They have not. They are using authorities Parliament and the courts left in place, more visibly and more often than they did fifteen years ago.

The change of degree is real. Departmental plans now carry numerical hiring goals. The Canadian Armed Forces published representation targets of 25 percent women, 3.5 percent Indigenous, and 11.8 percent visible minority among uniformed personnel. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada folded branch-level hiring goals into a 2022–25 diversity strategy. The Clerk’s Call to Action pushed deputies to show marked progress. The result is that language which used to live in human-resources manuals now sits in the first screen of a job ad, including ads for work the public reasonably expects to be staffed on competence first.

What the workforce numbers actually show

The official snapshots do not support a simple story that the public service is closed to minorities. As of March 31, 2025, women, Indigenous peoples, and visible minorities were at or above workforce availability in the core public administration. Persons with disabilities were not: roughly 9 percent representation against a 12 percent availability benchmark. The visible-minority share of new hires rose from 17.9 percent in 2016–17 to 27.2 percent in 2024–25.

Those aggregates hide occupational gaps. Technical, scientific, operational, and some executive ranks still show shortfalls for particular groups. They also hide the opposite problem: once a group has crossed the benchmark, the same tools remain available. A policy justified as gap-closing does not automatically switch off when the gap closes. That is a design question, not a talking point.

The 2018 Public Service Commission name-blind recruitment pilot is relevant here and usually omitted. In 27 processes and about 2,200 applicants, shortlisting rates for visible-minority candidates were statistically the same with and without names attached. That does not prove the absence of every barrier. It does weaken the claim that ordinary screening is so contaminated that identity preference is the only remaining instrument.

What a posting cannot prove

A sentence on a poster is evidence of intent and of process design. It is not evidence of who was hired. Self-declaration is voluntary except in restricted competitions. Many of the same posters also give preference to veterans, and sometimes to spouses of serving members. Departments do not publish, job by job, whether the organizational-need clause was invoked, how many qualified non-members were passed over, or whether the appointed person was the strongest candidate on the essential qualifications.

So the accurate statement is limited. Identity is now an advertised, lawful selection factor in a large minority of federal competitions. In a small minority it is a condition of applying. In safety and intelligence work, that fact is consequential because the public cannot easily observe quality and therefore relies on the integrity of the process. The posting language is enough to raise the question. It is not enough to answer it.

The live disagreement, stated without decoration

There are two serious positions. They should be kept apart from the slogans that usually travel with them.

One position says representation is a legitimate organizational need. A public service that serves a diverse country, and an armed forces that must recruit from the whole population, will underperform if whole groups are persistently absent from particular occupations. On this view, using identity among qualified candidates—or, in scarce cases, opening a competition only to an underrepresented group—is a permitted correction, not a betrayal of merit. Merit, in the statute, already includes organizational need.

The other position says that once essential qualifications are met, choosing by sex, race, Indigenous status, or disability is still choosing by a trait unrelated to the work. On this view, the harm is largest precisely where the work is least forgiving: inspecting aircraft, assessing intelligence, managing emergencies. A process that tells a qualified applicant that someone else’s identity may finish ahead of theirs will, over time, change who applies, who trusts the institution, and how errors are explained when they occur.

Both positions can cite law. Only the second treats the current definition of merit as the problem. Only the first treats workforce-availability matching as a sufficient reason to rank people. The data on aggregate representation makes the first position harder to maintain as an emergency measure and easier to maintain as a standing rule. That is the fork.

What people in the system should do with this

If you write these posters, stop treating the three bands as interchangeable. If a competition is open to everyone and identity is only a possible tie-breaker among the qualified, say that in words a candidate can understand. If the competition is closed, say it in the first line. Ambiguous “may” language maximizes legal cover and minimizes public comprehension.

If you oversee departments, require a recorded reason when the organizational-need clause is used: which gap, in which occupational group, against which benchmark, and why this appointment is the instrument. A target that cannot be turned off after it is met will not stay a gap-closing tool.

If you report on this, quote the poster. Distinguish restricted competitions from “may be based on.” Do not call four statutory groups “race.” Do not call a voluntary self-declaration box a quota. Do not call a published CAF percentage target a rumour.

If you oppose the practice, the strongest ground is not that it is secret or illegal. It is that it is now ordinary, disclosed, and applied to work where the cost of a weaker qualified pool is not theoretical. If you defend the practice, the strongest ground is not that critics are imagining the language. They are not. The ground is that the statute defines merit that way, the gaps in some occupations are real, and essential qualifications still come first.

The advertisements are not a hoax. They are also not a complete account of who gets the job. An honest public argument can live in that space. It does not need a larger claim than the page will bear.
