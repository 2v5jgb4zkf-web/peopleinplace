A session that could look

15 August 2026 · Shelby · Written

I click what I can see. I don’t live in keyboard shortcuts, and I’m tired of being treated like that’s a character flaw.

This afternoon I used a tool that didn’t need the usual act. It sits in a window. I talk to it in ordinary sentences. I asked it to look at the small new Mac I actually use — browser first, files in piles, Apple’s layout left alone — and to say, plainly, what I never touch, what I reach for, and how to put only the second group in front of me.

It could look. That was the useful bit. Not a lecture on how a Mac’s supposed to be used. It read the icons Apple had lined up for me, the screenshots covering the desktop, the download pile, the programs I’ve never opened, and the few I open all the time. The picture was ordinary: the web, messages, a place to write, photographs, something for films — and, almost easy to miss, the conversation I was already in.

The unused icons came off the bar. What’s left is short enough to read at a glance. The screenshots went into a folder of their own; new ones’ll land there instead of on the desktop. Downloads got sorted into the few headings a life actually produces. That was an afternoon of tidying, not a new career in filing.

A consumer antivirus that’d been popping up got checked instead of believed. It wasn’t protecting anything. It was only using memory and asking to be noticed. It came off. So did the month-box calendar Apple pins to the desktop, which I’ve never used as a calendar.

Then the small thing that matters: one icon on the bar that opens this tool. One click. No hunting, no command to remember, no need to become a different kind of person to keep talking. When an old window warned that closing it would “terminate things that were running,” I asked if that was true. It was only the conversation, already saved. The files were already on disk. Safe to close.

I’m not going to inventory a private machine in public. The furniture isn’t the point. The point’s the kind of help that was possible in one sitting for someone who won’t memorise a hidden language of keys.

I asked questions the way I would across a table. The machine got judged against my habits, not against a manufacturer’s idea of a good customer. The visible surface got brought into line with those habits. A nagging program got judged by what it was doing, not by what it claimed. I got a door back into the work that didn’t depend on memory. And at the end I asked for that sequence to be written down and put here — on this site — without handing strangers the contents of my drawers.

That last step’s the one I want to keep. This place exists so a thought can have a room. It’s also a place I can send a finished piece and have it set in the wall: not a feed, not a story that vanishes. A page with a date.

The test of a tool isn’t whether it makes you more like the people who built it. It’s whether it can meet you as you already work, and leave the desk clearer than it found it.

I still click what I can see. After this afternoon there’s less on the screen that doesn’t belong to me, and one more thing on the bar that does.
