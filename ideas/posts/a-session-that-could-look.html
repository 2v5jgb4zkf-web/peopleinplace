A session that could look

15 August 2026 · Shelby

I do not live in keyboard shortcuts. I click what I can see. That is not a failing; it is how most people actually use a computer. The industry prefers to pretend otherwise — to treat fluency as a kind of priesthood, and the rest of us as temporarily embarrassed experts.

This afternoon I sat with a tool that does not require that pretence. It runs in a window, in ordinary language. I asked it to look at the machine in front of me — a small new Mac I have been using as I use any computer: browser first, files in piles, the factory layout untouched — and to tell me, plainly, what I was not using, what I reached for, and how the desk might be rearranged so that only the latter remained in view.

It could look. That is the part worth marking. Not a list of tips. Not a lecture on how a Mac “should” be used. It read the bar of icons Apple had left for me, the wall of screenshots on the desktop, the drawer of downloads, the programs that had never been opened and the handful that had been opened hundreds of times. The picture was unsurprising to anyone who has watched themselves work: the web, the messages, a place to write, photographs, a screen for films — and, sitting almost invisibly, the conversation I was already in.

What we actually did

The unused icons came off the bar. What remains is short enough to read at a glance. The desktop was emptied of its screenshot drift; those images now live in a folder of their own, and new ones will land there instead of on the face of the machine. The download pile was sorted into ordinary rooms — work, home, the few other headings a life actually produces — without turning the afternoon into a filing career.

A consumer antivirus that had been sending pop-ups was inspected rather than trusted on reputation. It was no longer protecting anything. It was only occupying memory and asking to be noticed. It was removed. A calendar I have never used as a calendar — the month box Apple pins to the desktop — was taken down the same way.

Then the practical thing, which matters more than it sounds: a single icon, on the bar, that opens this tool. One tap. No hunting through folders, no remembering a command, no becoming a different kind of person in order to continue a conversation. When an older window warned that closing it would “terminate things that were running,” I could ask whether that was true. It was only the conversation itself, already saved. The files were already on disk. Safe to close.

What I am willing to say in public

I am not going to inventory a private machine on a public site. The point is not the furniture. The point is the kind of help that became possible in one sitting, for someone who will not — and should not have to — memorise a hidden language of keys.

I could ask a question in the same voice I would use across a table. I could have the machine inspected against my actual habits rather than against a manufacturer’s idea of a good customer. I could have the visible surface of the computer brought into line with those habits. I could have a nagging program judged by what it was doing, not by what it claimed. I could get a door back into the work that did not depend on memory. And I could ask, at the end, for that sequence to be written down and placed here — on this site — in a form that does not hand strangers the contents of my drawers.

That last step is the one I want to keep. This place was opened so that thought would have a room of its own. It is also, now, a place I can send a finished thought and have it set in the wall: not a feed, not a story that vanishes, a page with a date.

The useful test of a tool is not whether it makes you more like the people who built it. It is whether it can meet you as you already work — and leave the desk clearer than it found it.

I am still a person who clicks what he can see. After this afternoon, there is less to see that does not belong to me, and one more thing on the bar that does.
