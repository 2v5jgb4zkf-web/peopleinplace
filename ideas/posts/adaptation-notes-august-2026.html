What’s new in adaptation

17 August 2026 · Shelby

Mid-August 2026. A collation of the Government of Canada’s first National Adaptation Strategy Progress Report — the official 115-page report (PDF) on the 2023 National Adaptation Strategy. This note does not replace that record. Names and figures below link to the source pages.

The frame

The Strategy is Canada’s first whole-of-society framework for climate risk: disaster, nature, health, infrastructure, and the economy. Since fall 2022 the federal government has committed more than $2.1 billion to implementation. Budget 2025 added community-infrastructure and youth initiatives. Recent delivery includes 141 climate-resilience projects in 128 communities through the Green Municipal Fund’s Local Leadership for Climate Adaptation initiative. The report repeats the research finding that every dollar invested in adaptation can save up to $15 in avoided costs.

Progress is reported across all five systems. Climate impacts continue to intensify. Canada is warming roughly twice as fast as the global average — three times as fast in the North. The monitoring framework has expanded from 13 indicators to 47. Near-term targets on awareness, climate services, and decision-making have largely been met. Deeper implementation gaps remain. The Government of Canada Adaptation Action Plan is advancing 73 actions across 22 departments. See also the monitoring and evaluation framework.

Disaster resilience

Public awareness of household climate risks reached 92 percent (target: 60 percent by 2025). More than 480 communities now have flood-hazard mapping under the Flood Hazard Identification and Mapping Program (target: more than 1,000 by 2028). Disaster Financial Assistance Arrangements were modernized and launched 1 April 2025, with stronger incentives for risk reduction. Forty-two communities have “FireSmart Ready” status. The Disaster Mitigation and Adaptation Fund is supporting 149 projects, including Bay of Fundy dikes protecting 60 communities.

Gaps: only about 30 percent of households have taken concrete risk-reduction measures (missed the 50 percent target). A National Recovery Strategy is still in early development (due 2028).

Health and well-being

All health regions have implemented evidence-based measures to protect health from extreme heat (target: 80 percent by 2026; met in 2024–25). Climate-change health adaptation projects cover 50.8 percent of First Nations and Inuit communities; 92–94 percent of recipients report improved adaptive capacity. In 2024–25 there were 158 new or enhanced systems and tools for climate-sensitive infectious diseases. Air Quality Health Index risk communications cover 80 percent of the population.

Gaps: many indicators — vulnerability assessments, heat-alert coverage, heat-related mortality tracking — first report in 2026–27. The target to eliminate heat-related deaths remains 2040.

Nature and biodiversity

Ecological-corridor support exceeded its target: 27 partner-led projects (10 Indigenous-led) against a minimum of 3 by 2026. A Force of Nature: Canada’s Strategy to Protect Nature was released in March 2026. The Nature Smart Climate Solutions Fund, the Natural Climate Solutions Fund, and the 2 Billion Trees program are in delivery. Marine conservation stood at 15.5 percent and terrestrial conserved area at 14 percent at the end of 2024.

Gaps: limited progress toward the 25 percent terrestrial / 30 percent marine conservation targets by 2025/2030. Ongoing forest conversion. Uncertain status of many fish stocks.

Infrastructure

In 2025 Canada became the first country to embed future climate design values in the National Building Code. Resilience was also written into the Highway Bridge Design Code and the Electrical Code. The Climate Toolkit for Housing and Infrastructure (October 2024) supported more than 450 communities (73 Indigenous) in its first year. ClimateInsight.ca passed 100,000 visits. Since April 2024, all federal infrastructure funding programs include resilience-by-design requirements. Significant investment continues through the Disaster Mitigation and Adaptation Fund, the National Trade Corridors Fund ($4.1 billion for 214 projects), and First Nations infrastructure programs.

Gaps: municipal adaptation cost estimates around $5.3 billion a year. Capacity, data, and funding barriers remain sharpest for rural, northern, and Indigenous communities. Short-term funding cycles and low-upfront-cost procurement still get in the way.

Economy and workers

The report cites projected annual losses of $78–101 billion by mid-century without stronger adaptation. Financial regulators, including OSFI, now require climate-hazard mapping and risk integration. Sector work is advancing in energy (wildfire frameworks), forestry (FireSmart, growth models), mining, transportation, and tourism. Workforce pieces include the Federal Sustainable Jobs Action Plan, a $40-million Youth Climate Corps, and regional training such as CLIMAtlantic.

Gaps: shortages of data and tools for extreme events. Skill gaps — about 44 percent of engineers and 52 percent of architects report adequate climate-adaptation competence. Limited Northern data. Supply-chain vulnerability shown in costly disruptions at ports and corridors.

Foundation and what has risen since 2023

Climate-services access reached 295,068 users in 2024–25, against a 2024 target of 180,000. Public alerting covers a large majority of the population. Access to data, tools, and Indigenous knowledge systems is still uneven.

New priority areas named since the Strategy’s release: food and water security; defence and national security; displacement; financial and economic resilience; housing; and the role of artificial intelligence in both risk and adaptation.

The official conclusion is that the foundation is stronger and several near-term targets have been met or exceeded — and that the pace of climate change still outstrips current effort. Gaps remain between planning and implementation, between regions with capacity and those without, and between the scale of risk and the scale of investment.
