What’s new in restoration

16 August 2026 · Shelby

Mid-August 2026. A weekly collation of ecological restoration moving across Canada — landscape connectivity, estuaries, wetlands, kelp, municipal nature-based work, and Indigenous-led stewardship. Names and figures below link to the project pages and announcements.

Landscape scale

Near Pukaskwa National Park, the Nature Conservancy of Canada has secured more than 8,100 hectares of boreal forest, wetland, and shoreline at Kabenung Lakes and Forests. The work spans three watersheds and sixteen fish-bearing lakes, including fire-resistant mixed-wood forest. Aims include water quality for downstream communities such as Wawa; movement for moose, lynx, wolves, and black bear; habitat for more than twelve species at risk; traditional harvesting; and some buffer against flood and fire. Partners include Michipicoten First Nation, Netmizaaggaming Nishnaabeg, Biigtigong Nishnaabeg, and Parks Canada, through the Landscape Resiliency Program.

On Vancouver Island, the Province of B.C. has put $500,000 toward restoring about seventy hectares of marsh in the Cowichan / Quw’utsun / Xwulqw’selu estuary — the largest estuary restoration on the Island. Work is to remove dikes and barriers, reconnect tidal process, restore salmon, shellfish, and wildlife habitat, and give the shore a better chance against flood, surge, and sea-level rise. Without it, as much as sixty percent of the remaining marsh is at risk. Partners include Nature Trust of BC, Cowichan Tribes, the Province, Ducks Unlimited Canada, the Habitat Conservation Trust Foundation, Fisheries and Oceans Canada, and Environment and Climate Change Canada. Channel excavation, a native plant nursery, and cultural plantings are in the plan, with completion aimed at the end of 2028. See also the project science and updates.

In Howe Sound / Átl’ka7tsem, BCIT, with Woodfibre LNG and West Coast Kelp, has launched a multi-year kelp restoration pilot. The method is spore collection, nursery growth, seeding on ropes, then transfer onto rock reef and cobble. It is separate from earlier Ocean Wise and Squamish Nation work in the same sound. Cycles run over about five years.

Wetlands and local ground

Ontario continues to fund wetlands through the Wetlands Conservation Partner Program and the Species Conservation Program. On 10 August the province announced nearly $1.2 million for the Heart Lake Road Wetlands Connectivity Project in Brampton — about ten hectares inside the Heart Lake Wetlands Complex. Shoreline work, removal of invasives such as common buckthorn, native planting, and stormwater upgrades, with the City of Brampton and the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority. The award sits inside a larger provincial allocation of about $9.6 million. Ontario reports nearly $41 million into this work since 2020, restoring or enhancing more than 12,200 acres.

The City of Oshawa and CLOCA have $1,008,460 in Species Conservation funding for multi-year habitat work at Second Marsh — Least Bittern, Blanding’s Turtle, American Eel, and climate resilience — with Ducks Unlimited Canada, Friends of Second Marsh, and the Hamilton-Oshawa Port Authority.

In Mississauga, a federal-municipal partnership of more than $680,000 will restore about 1.1 hectares of floodplain forest and wetland at Iggy Kaneff Park along Cooksville Creek: a thousand trees, six thousand shrubs, constructed shallow wetlands, and treatment of invasives. The Natural Infrastructure Fund contribution is about $453,000. Samuel de Champlain Provincial Park continues post-wildfire planting with climate-informed species. South Glengarry has finished shoreline work at Glen Walter Waterfront Park with Watersheds Canada.

Municipal adaptation

On 12 August, Environment and Climate Change Canada and the Federation of Canadian Municipalities announced $34.2 million for 141 projects through the Green Municipal Fund’s Local Leadership for Climate Adaptation initiative. Nature-based examples include Capital Regional District shoreline restoration at Coles Bay (about $898,200); a City of Vancouver wetland restoration study at Kinross Ravine Park; ecological restoration with the City of Courtenay and Comox Valley Project Watershed Society; nature-based shoreline options at French Creek Estuary in the Regional District of Nanaimo; Halalt First Nation floodplain restoration feasibility on the Lower Chemainus; and community vegetation work through Habitat Acquisition Trust. The wider LLCA program is about $530 million. Further implementation windows are expected in October.

Indigenous-led work

Cowichan Tribes are partners in estuary stewardship, cultural plants, and food systems. Kabenung is being planned with Anishinaabe Nations. In northeastern B.C., Blueberry River First Nations unveiled a native plant orchard in mid-July to supply seedlings for industrial and wildfire sites. Osoyoos Indian Band continues wildfire forest restoration with native species. Tłı̨chǫ tree-planting on traditional lands in the Northwest Territories is multi-year, locally sourced, and aimed at both restoration and wildfire protection. Earlier Indigenous-Led Natural Climate Solutions Fund projects — Nazko wetlands, Kwiakah forestry, Whitefish Lake mapping — remain in delivery.

Funding

During Canadian Environment Week in June, the Environmental Damages Fund allocated more than $4 million to twelve projects in Quebec, New Brunswick, and Newfoundland and Labrador. Examples include riverbank and aquatic habitat work on the Restigouche with the Gespe’gewa’gi Institute of Natural Understanding, and eelgrass work at Baie des Capucins. The fund itself remains the place to watch for later rounds. In August the federal government launched an Expert Taskforce on Natural Capital Accounting and Nature Financing. Broader delivery continues under A Force of Nature — salmon habitat, species at risk, and Indigenous stewardship.
