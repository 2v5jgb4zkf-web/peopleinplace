What’s new in restoration

16 August 2026 · Shelby

This is a sharing note, not an argument. Restoration work in this country moves in many rooms at once — federal strategies, provincial pots, municipal adaptation money, land trusts, and Nations doing the actual stewardship. Here is what has been announced or advanced in the last few weeks, as of mid-August 2026.

Three places that set the scale

In northern Ontario, near Pukaskwa National Park, the Nature Conservancy of Canada has secured more than 8,100 hectares of boreal forest, wetland, and shoreline at Kabenung Lakes and Forests. The announcement landed around 11–14 August. It is a connectivity project across three watersheds and sixteen fish-bearing lakes, including fire-resistant mixed-wood forest. The stated aims are water quality for downstream communities such as Wawa, wildlife movement for moose, lynx, wolves, and black bear, habitat for more than twelve species at risk, traditional harvesting, and some buffer against flood and fire. NCC is working with Michipicoten First Nation, Netmizaaggaming Nishnaabeg, Biigtigong Nishnaabeg, and Parks Canada. It sits inside the Landscape Resiliency Program.

On Vancouver Island, the province put $500,000 toward restoring about seventy hectares of marsh in the Cowichan / Quw’utsun / Xwulqw’selu estuary — the largest estuary restoration on the Island. That money was announced 24 July; local debate over farmland impacts was still in the papers in early August. The work is to take out dikes and barriers, reconnect tidal process, restore salmon, shellfish, and wildlife habitat, and give the shore a better chance against flood, surge, and sea-level rise. Without it, as much as sixty percent of the remaining marsh is at risk. Partners include Nature Trust of BC, Cowichan Tribes, the Province, Ducks Unlimited Canada, the Habitat Conservation Trust Foundation, Fisheries and Oceans Canada, and Environment and Climate Change Canada. Channel excavation, a native plant nursery, and cultural plantings are in the plan. Completion is aimed at the end of 2028.

In Howe Sound / Átl’ka7tsem, BCIT, with support from Woodfibre LNG and West Coast Kelp, launched a multi-year kelp restoration pilot around 12 August. The method is spore collection, nursery growth, seeding on ropes, then transfer onto rock reef and cobble. It is separate from earlier Ocean Wise and Squamish Nation work. The point is to learn what holds, in cycles, over about five years.

Wetlands and local ground

Ontario is still putting money into wetlands through the Wetlands Conservation Partner Program and the Species Conservation Program. On 10 August the province announced nearly $1.2 million for the Heart Lake Road Wetlands Connectivity Project in Brampton — about ten hectares inside the Heart Lake Wetlands Complex. Shoreline work, removal of invasives such as common buckthorn, native planting, and stormwater upgrades, with the City of Brampton and the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority. That award sits inside a larger provincial allocation of about $9.6 million. Ontario says it has put nearly $41 million into this work since 2020, restoring or enhancing more than 12,200 acres.

A day later, about $1.008 million in Species Conservation funding went to Oshawa Second Marsh for multi-year habitat work for Least Bittern, Blanding’s Turtle, and American Eel, and for climate resilience. Partners: the City of Oshawa, Central Lake Ontario Conservation Authority, Ducks Unlimited Canada, Friends of Second Marsh, and the Hamilton-Oshawa Port Authority. It sits alongside earlier federal Great Lakes money.

In Mississauga, more than $680,000 in a federal-municipal partnership — announced in July — will restore about 1.1 hectares of floodplain forest and wetland at Iggy Kaneff Park: a thousand trees, six thousand shrubs, constructed shallow wetlands, and treatment of invasives. The Natural Infrastructure Fund is in for about $453,000. Samuel de Champlain Provincial Park continues post-wildfire planting with climate-informed species. South Glengarry finished shoreline work at Glen Walter Waterfront Park with Watersheds Canada.

Municipal adaptation money

On 12 August the Green Municipal Fund’s Local Leadership for Climate Adaptation program announced $34.2 million for 141 projects. A good number of them are nature-based: shoreline, wetland, vegetation, floodplain. Among them: Capital Regional District shoreline restoration at Coles Bay (about $898,200); a City of Vancouver wetland restoration study at Kinross Ravine Park; ecological restoration with the City of Courtenay and Comox Valley Project Watershed Society; nature-based shoreline options at French Creek Estuary in the Regional District of Nanaimo; Halalt First Nation floodplain restoration feasibility on the Lower Chemainus; and community vegetation work through Habitat Acquisition Trust. Thunder Bay and other municipalities are in the same stream for stormwater and flood-resilient ground.

Nations leading the work

Cowichan Tribes are not a footnote on the estuary project. They are in the stewardship, the cultural plants, and the food system. The Kabenung work is being planned with Anishinaabe Nations. In northeastern B.C., Blueberry River First Nations unveiled a native plant orchard in mid-July to supply seedlings for industrial and wildfire sites, with the Blueberry Restoration Society. Osoyoos Indian Band continues wildfire forest restoration with native species. Tłı̨chǫ tree-planting on traditional lands in the Northwest Territories is multi-year, locally sourced, and aimed at both restoration and wildfire protection. Cultural burning and regenerative forestry examples continue on Vancouver Island and elsewhere. Earlier Indigenous-Led Natural Climate Solutions Fund projects — Nazko wetlands, Kwiakah forestry, Whitefish Lake mapping — are still being delivered, not just announced.

The money around it

The Green Municipal Fund LLCA pot is part of a program totalling about $530 million. The Environmental Damages Fund put more than $4 million into twelve projects in Quebec, New Brunswick, and Newfoundland and Labrador around Canadian Environment Week in June; those are now in delivery. Examples include riverbank and aquatic habitat work on the Restigouche with the Gespe’gewa’gi Institute of Natural Understanding, and eelgrass work at Baie des Capucins. An earlier EDF round, in December 2025, was about $10.6 million across twenty-three projects. In August the federal government also launched an Expert Taskforce on Natural Capital Accounting and Nature Financing, aimed at bringing private capital into this kind of work. Broader federal nature-strategy money — salmon habitat, species at risk, Indigenous stewardship — is the backdrop most of these projects sit against.

What to watch next

Cowichan moves from announcement into construction. NCC will have to turn the Kabenung securement into a living stewardship plan. Further LLCA windows are expected around October. Environmental Damages Fund and provincial wetland and species programs remain open in the usual way, often with matching dollars required. The useful test is not the press release. It is whether the dikes come out, the plants take, the corridors actually hold, and the people who live with the ground stay in the work after the announcement cycle moves on.
