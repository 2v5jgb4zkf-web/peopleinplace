What we can rank, and what we can actually touch

9 September 2026 · Shelby · Directed

A new paper on paracetamol in pregnancy is making the usual circuit. The Globe and Mail carried the Reuters version this week: mothers’ use of Tylenol, or the generic equivalent, associated with smaller ovaries and uteruses and fewer follicles in infant girls. The underlying study is Fischer and colleagues’ COPANA cohort in Human Reproduction Open — prospective diaries, urine measures, ultrasounds at three months, plus a second Danish group followed into adolescence. The authors themselves say it does not prove the drug caused the differences, and it does not show what those girls’ fertility will look like twenty years from now. That is a fair place to start, and a better place than the headline.

Globe · paper · Reuters

If you sit with it for a minute, the interesting question is not “is the world poisoned.” It is closer to: when a finding like this arrives, what else already belongs in the same drawer, and which of those things can a person actually move?

Reproductive biology is not a single switch. Eggs are allocated once, in fetal life. Sperm are rebuilt on a roughly three-month cycle. Heat, hormones, infection, smoke, and metabolic state all lean on that machinery in different ways. So a chemical signal in pregnancy and a sauna habit in a man trying to conceive are not the same kind of problem, even if they both show up in a conversation about “reproductive health.” One cannot be rerun. The other often can.

Look at the large, repeated human evidence and a ranking starts to appear without much ideology attached. Tobacco tracks with poorer semen quality, more sperm DNA damage, and faster follicle loss. Body weight — too much or, in women, too little — changes ovulation and sperm parameters; modest loss in people with obesity often restores cycles. Heavy drinking is a different story from a glass of wine. Exogenous testosterone and anabolic steroids are among the most predictable pharmaceutical suppressors of sperm production. Untreated infection scars tubes and epididymides. The testes prefer to work a few degrees cooler than the rest of the body, which is why occupational heat, hot tubs, and heat waves keep showing up in semen data. None of that requires a conspiracy. It is physiology under load.

Mayo Clinic, sperm · NICHD, lifestyle

Then there is the slower background: air pollution, phthalates, bisphenols, PFAS. Recent reviews in endocrinology and a FIGO committee opinion treat these as plausible contributors to ovarian reserve, semen quality, and some of the disorders that have become more common, while still admitting that human causality is hard to lock down. A 2026 meta-analysis bundled PM2.5, NO2, BPA, and phthalates with worse semen and ovarian markers. That is a real second layer. It is also the layer most people meet as a feeling of helplessness — the receipt, the can liner, the water main — which is how a thought exercise turns into either panic or dismissal.

Nature Reviews Endocrinology, 2025 · FIGO opinion

The Tylenol paper fits that second layer more than the first. There is older lab work suggesting acetaminophen can bother steroid production and germ cells in developing ovaries. COPANA is one of the cleaner human looks at the female side. It is still observational. Women take the drug because they have pain or fever; those conditions are not inert. Obstetric colleges have not, on the back of this study, withdrawn acetaminophen as first-line treatment when fever or pain is actually present. Untreated high fever in pregnancy has its own record. The practical sentence that survives all of that is a modest one: use what is needed, at the lowest dose that works, for the shortest time, and do not confuse a signal worth studying with a reason to suffer a dangerous fever.

CNN on clinical caution · ACOG · SOGC

So the thought exercise is really about jurisdiction. Some exposures are collective — the airshed, the water chemistry, the composition of food packaging. Those want regulation and patience. Some are closer to the body you live in this year: whether you smoke, how much mass you carry, whether “TRT for energy” is a story you have been sold, whether the testes spend the evening in a hot tub, whether last night’s food is reheated in the same warped plastic it arrived in, whether a painkiller is doing a job or just keeping company. The second list is smaller and less cinematic. It is also the list on which a person can change the next three months.

You can walk that list without becoming a hermit in a jar pantry. Glass or steel for hot food when it is easy. Leave the greasy thermal receipt in the bin. Treat body weight as a lever if it is one. Do not perform purity while ignoring smoke. If a clinician has already named a medicine as acceptable in pregnancy, the finding above is a reason for proportion, not a reason to switch to something with a worse pregnancy record.

The supplement aisle will always offer a more glamorous plot: the threat is everywhere, therefore the fix must be purchased. A quieter reading of the same evidence is that ranking still works. Put the large, owned, often reversible loads first. Keep the chemical background in view without letting it eat the whole frame. Leave room for a drug when the drug is doing real work. Then live with the decision, which is the only test that tends to last.
