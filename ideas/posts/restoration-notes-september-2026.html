What’s new in restoration

6 September 2026 · Shelby · Generated

Early September 2026. What has moved since the mid-August note — a new reserve on the Exploits, field work on Wash Brook and on Tłı̨chǫ ground, a UNESCO visit to Wood Buffalo, and another round of New Brunswick community money. Names and figures link to the source pages.

What moved

On 27 August the Nature Conservancy of Canada, Teck Resources, and the Government of Canada announced the Beothuk Lake Nature Reserve near Millertown, Newfoundland and Labrador. Sixteen hundred hectares on both sides of the Exploits River and along the south shore of Beothuk Lake: boreal forest, wetlands, riparian ground, Atlantic salmon habitat. Less than three percent of the Central Newfoundland Forest Ecoregion is protected. Teck put in $2 million. Canada contributed through the Natural Heritage Conservation Program. NCC bought the land from Domtar for $1.3 million. Stewardship planning is next. The ground sits beside Indian Point National Historic Site, a Beothuk winter camp and caribou hunting ground. CBC

In Sydney, ACAP Cape Breton is in the field on Wash Brook — the first large-scale restoration along that corridor after years of flood damage. Crews are taking out multiflora rose, wild angelica, and purple loosestrife. In September the brook is to be widened and planted with natives, including milkweed and white turtlehead. Heavy work through the fall near the public gardens and Legends Arena, then years of monitoring. The work sits beside the Washbrook Greenway Trail.

On Tłı̨chǫ land, the remote piece of this year’s plant landed. A crew of about forty-five flew into Wekweètì in early September to put 425,000 black and white spruce on a burn along the south shore of Snare Lake, over two weeks. Local boat drivers, bear guards, and the culture camp as lodging. The larger Tłı̨chǫ plan remains 13 million locally sourced trees by 2030 — restoration, caribou habitat, and a fire buffer around the communities.

At Wood Buffalo National Park, the third UNESCO / IUCN reactive monitoring mission finished its in-person work on 23 August. Delegates walked the Peace-Athabasca Delta, recent burn, and the new $10.7 million Field Station. The file is still cumulative industrial pressure on the Peace and Athabasca, wood bison and whooping crane, and shared governance with the eleven Indigenous Nations and organizations tied to the park. Mikisew Cree leadership has said monitoring is not the same as protection. The mission report goes to the World Heritage Committee in Istanbul in June–July 2027. Force of Nature money already on the books: $90 million over five years for the Action Plan and two wood bison populations on the Alberta–NWT line.

On 2 September New Brunswick announced more than $1.1 million from the Environmental Trust Fund for 30 southern projects, including long work on Ritchie Lake in Quispamsis. That is the third regional drop this cycle. The full 2026–27 pot is $7.9 million across 173 projects. Groups are already asking what replaces the fund when the remaining balance runs down. A “what we heard” paper on modernization came out with the announcement. Midterm reports for possible recurring projects are due 30 September.

Still moving

Iggy Kaneff Park on Cooksville Creek. Blueberry River’s native plant orchard. Osoyoos Indian Band fire-forest work. The June Environmental Damages Fund awards in Québec, New Brunswick, and Newfoundland and Labrador.
